\begin{abstract}
The evaluation of 3D face reconstruction is typically based on measuring
geometric distances between reconstructed and ground-truth faces after rigid or
non-rigid alignment. These alignment steps are useful for improving surface overlap, but they introduce a non-uniform bias that distorts the overall similarity structure. As a result, error values become inconsistent across different mesh templates, resolutions, and topologies. We study this problem as a metric meta-evaluation task. Using topology-consistent synthetic faces with known correspondences, we compute true geometric errors and test whether registration-based pipelines preserve the correct ranking of identity differences when topology, sampling, support, and rigid perturbations change. Using a controlled cross-topology benchmark (REMESH), we show that rigid and non-rigid ICP compress the range of distances between subjects. They improve pairwise local surface alignments but reduce identity-related differences, leading to worse ranking consistency. This suggests that reliable 3D face evaluation should separate pairwise alignment quality from identity-aware similarity. To address this, we propose an encoder-only, mesh-agnostic learned metric that maps 3D face meshes into a shared latent space designed to preserve ground-truth dissimilarities using stress, ranking, and identity-consistency objectives. At test time, each mesh is encoded independently and compared in this latent space, without pairwise ICP, non-rigid registration, landmarks, or dense correspondence. On the REMESH benchmark, the proposed metric consistently preserves ground-truth rankings better than standard pipelines.

\section{Introduction}
Reconstructing 3D faces from single or multiple images is a long-standing problem in computer vision. Recent methods can produce increasingly realistic and detailed facial geometry, even under challenging imaging conditions~\cite{3ddfav2, 3di, deca, deep3d, mica, synergynet, emoca, inorig}. However, as these methods become more diverse in template, topology, resolution, and preprocessing, one key question becomes more critical: \emph{how should we measure reconstruction quality when predicted meshes are not directly comparable?}
%Reconstructing 3D faces from single or multiple images is a long-standing problem in computer vision, with recent methods producing increasingly plausible and detailed facial geometry across challenging imaging conditions~\cite{3ddfav2, 3di, deca, deep3d, mica, synergynet, emoca, inorig}. As reconstruction methods become more diverse in template, topology, resolution, and preprocessing, however, a basic question becomes increasingly important: \emph{how should reconstruction quality be measured when predicted meshes are not directly comparable?}

Standard evaluation protocols, \textit{e.g.}~\cite{now,REALY}, address this question by computing geometric distances, typically the Chamfer distance, after alignment and preprocessing steps. These steps may include rigid alignment, rigid ICP, non-rigid ICP, cropping, landmark initialization, or point-to-surface distance computation. The goal is to make different meshes comparable by enforcing dense correspondence. While these steps are useful, they introduce an important limitation. They optimize pairwise surface alignment, but not the global similarity structure across all meshes; as a result, similarities and differences across the full set of identities may not be preserved. In particular, alignment can reduce geometric differences that carry identity information, such as facial depth, profile shape, or local protrusions. Thus, better pairwise fit does not necessarily mean better identity-aware ranking.
%Standard evaluation protocols \textit{e.g.}~\cite{now,REALY} answer this question by computing a geometric distance (typically the Chamfer distance) after some form of pre-processing and alignment procedures. Rigid alignment, ICP, non-rigid ICP, cropping, landmark initialization, and point-to-surface distances are used to make heterogeneous meshes comparable and serve to establish a dense point-correspondence before measuring error. These procedures are useful, but they also introduce a subtle problem: they optimize pairwise surface fit, not necessarily the global similarity structure required to compare many subjects consistently. In particular, an alignment that reduces the distance between two individual meshes may also suppress identity-discriminative morphology, such as facial depth, profile shape, or local protrusions. Therefore, a better pairwise geometric fit does not necessarily imply a better identity-aware ranking fidelity.

This distinction is important because the true geometric error between two arbitrary 3D face meshes is generally unknown~\cite{10448898}. A well-defined error exists only under strict conditions: meshes must share the same topology, discretization, and canonical alignment. In this setting, distances can be computed directly between corresponding vertices, for example using point-wise RMSE. Synthetic or topology-consistent 3D face data provides this setting. It allows us to \emph{meta-evaluate} metrics by measuring whether they preserve the ground-truth ordering of identity differences when mesh representation, sampling, support, or pose change. Our experiments show that this problem is not only theoretical. Using a controlled benchmark of synthetic faces derived from a 3D Morphable Model, called REMESH, we observe that standard geometric distances behave inconsistently across mesh types. Raw Chamfer distance is sensitive to topology mismatch. Rigid and non-rigid registration often worsen the agreement with ground-truth identity rankings. We find that this degradation is partly caused by distance compression: registration reduces the spread of inter-subject distances, making different identities appear more similar than they should.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}
\paragraph{3D face reconstruction evaluation.}
Several solutions have been proposed to evaluate 3D face reconstruction accuracy, most of which work by computing the average point-to-point nearest-neighbor distance after either rigid registration via ICP~\cite{jackson17,deng2019accurate,gecer2019ganfit,gecer2021fast,genova18,Bai_2021_CVPR, prnet, feng2018evaluation, sariyanidi20, shang20} or landmarks~\cite{liu2018disentangling,zhu2020beyond}, or non-rigid alternatives~\cite{ferrari2021sparse,booth20163d,gilani2017dense}.
NoW~\cite{now} evaluates reconstructed meshes by applying landmark-initialized rigid alignment to ground-truth scans and then computing scan-to-mesh distances. This makes the protocol applicable to methods with different output templates, but the resulting error depends on rigid registration quality and closest-point surface matching. 
REALY~\cite{REALY}, instead, uses a structured, region-aware non-rigid registration protocol to obtain localized reconstruction errors. While this provides a more detailed geometric assessment, it requires stronger assumptions on topology, region definitions, and preprocessing. These benchmarks standardize evaluation, but they treat registration-based geometric error as the main proxy for reconstruction quality.

\paragraph{Metric meta-evaluation.}
A complementary line of work asks whether the metrics used by benchmarks are themselves reliable. Synthetic or topology-consistent 3D face data makes this possible because dense correspondences are known, allowing estimated errors to be compared against correspondence-based reference distances. Sariyanidi et al.~\cite{10448898} and M3DFB~\cite{fg2025} followed this meta-evaluation perspective, showing that different alignment and registration pipelines can produce substantially different estimates of reconstruction error, making such assessment unreliable in practice. Our work builds on this idea, but shifts the focus from estimating local geometric error to preserving global identity-level rankings across heterogeneous mesh representations.

\paragraph{Geometric and learned distances.}
Geometric distances such as point-to-point, point-to-surface, and Chamfer distance are simple and widely used, but they are sensitive to sampling density, partial support, topology changes, and residual misalignment~\cite{achlioptas2018learning,nguyen2021point}. Other distances were investigated that are irrespective of mesh parameterization or rotations to eliminate the need for alignment, such as the varifold metric~\cite{kaltenmark2017general, pierson20223d}. However, a kernel needs to be defined to induce the metric on the shapes, which might have varying properties. Learned distances for point clouds, such as DPDist~\cite{urbach2020dpdist}, offer a data-driven alternative to hand-designed geometric metrics, while feature-space metrics in 2D vision, such as LPIPS~\cite{lpips}, show that learned representations can better capture task-relevant similarity than raw signal-space errors. However, learned metrics also depend on their training distribution and are rarely evaluated under controlled topology shifts with known 3D correspondences. In contrast, we train and evaluate a mesh-agnostic learned metric specifically for ranking-preserving 3D face evaluation, and explicitly compare it against registration-based geometric pipelines.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}
We consider the problem of evaluating a distance function between 3D faces.
Given two observed meshes $X_i$ and $X_j$, a candidate metric produces an estimated dissimilarity
%
%\begin{equation}
$D_{\mathrm{ES}}(X_i,X_j) \in \mathbb{R}_{\ge 0}$.
%\label{eq:estimated_dissimilarity}
%\end{equation}

For standard geometric pipelines, $D_{\mathrm{ES}}$ may correspond to Chamfer distance, rigidly aligned point-to-point distance, or non-rigidly registered point-to-surface distance. For the proposed method, $D_{\mathrm{ES}}$ is the Euclidean distance between learned latent embeddings.

In general, the true geometric discrepancy between two arbitrary 3D face meshes is not directly observable. As the meshes may differ in topology, resolution, sampling density, surface support, or pose, the correct point correspondence is unknown. We therefore follow a metric meta-evaluation setting. Starting from topology-consistent synthetic identities with known dense correspondence, we define a reference correspondence-based identity dissimilarity
%
\begin{equation}
D_{\mathrm{GT}}(i,j)
=
\sqrt{
\frac{1}{N}
\sum_{k=1}^{N}
\left\|
v_{i,k} - v_{j,k}
\right\|_2^2
}, 
\label{eq:ground_truth_distance}
\end{equation}

where $v_{i,k}$ and $v_{j,k}$ are corresponding vertices of index $k$ of subjects $i$ and $j$. This distance is used only as a reference for supervision and meta-evaluation; it is not assumed to be available in practice.

Given a set of held-out subjects $\mathcal S$, we compare a candidate distance matrix $D_{\mathrm{ES}}$ against the reference matrix $D_{\mathrm{GT}}$ over all cross-subject pairs. Our primary criterion is Spearman rank correlation,
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\mathrm{S}}
=
\operatorname{corr}_{\mathrm{Spearman}}
\left(
\{D_{\mathrm{ES}}(i,j)\}_{i<j},
\{D_{\mathrm{GT}}(i,j)\}_{i<j}
\right), 
\label{eq:spearman_correlation}
\end{equation}
which measures whether the estimated metric preserves the ordering induced by the ground-truth identity dissimilarities. This is the central requirement in our setting: if subject $i$ is closer to subject $j$ than to subject $k$ under the canonical correspondence-based geometry, a reliable metric should preserve this ordering even when the observed meshes differ in topology, sampling, or pose.

As a secondary criterion, we report Pearson correlation,
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\mathrm{P}}
=
\operatorname{corr}_{\mathrm{Pearson}}
\left(
\{D_{\mathrm{ES}}(i,j)\}_{i<j},
\{D_{\mathrm{GT}}(i,j)\}_{i<j}
\right), 
\label{eq:pearson_correlation}
\end{equation}
which measures linear agreement and therefore captures whether relative distance proportions are preserved beyond ranking. Throughout the paper, higher correlation indicates better agreement with the correspondence-based reference.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Mesh-Agnostic Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}
We learn a shared embedding space for heterogeneous 3D face meshes. By mesh-agnostic, we mean that the encoder does not require a fixed vertex count, mesh connectivity, or template correspondence across inputs. Given an input mesh $M$, an encoder $f_\theta$ maps it to a latent vector $z=f_\theta(M)\in\mathbb{R}^d$. The learned dissimilarity between two meshes is defined as Euclidean distance in latent space:
\begin{equation}
D_{\theta}(M_i,M_j)
=
\left\|
f_{\theta}(M_i)-f_{\theta}(M_j)
\right\|_2 .
\end{equation}
Unlike registration-based geometric pipelines, this distance does not require pairwise alignment, ICP, non-rigid registration, landmarks, or dense correspondence at inference time.

We use an encoder-only surface network based on DiffusionNet~\cite{diffusionet}. 
For each triangular mesh $M=(V,F)$, vertices are centered and normalized, and the network receives normalized XYZ coordinates together with precomputed geometric operators, including mass, Laplacian, eigensystem, and gradient operators. These operators provide a geometry-aware bias that helps generalization across mesh connectivity and sampling changes. Since the encoder also receives XYZ coordinates, it is not guaranteed to be exactly invariant to rigid transformations; robustness to rotation and translation is instead encouraged by cross-topology training and online perturbation augmentation.

The reported model uses four DiffusionNet blocks with hidden width 128 and outputs a 256-dimensional latent embedding. Vertex features are aggregated by concatenated mean and max pooling followed by a linear projection. The same encoder is shared across all topology variants.
\subsection{Training Objective}
\label{sec:training_objective}

Training uses topology-consistent synthetic identities with an exact subject-level
ground-truth distance matrix $D_{\mathrm{GT}}$. Each mini-batch contains multiple
subjects and multiple mesh realizations per subject. Let
$z_{s,m}=f_\theta(x_{s,m})$ be the embedding of realization $m$ of subject $s$,
and define the batch subject embedding as
$\bar z_s = M_s^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M_s} z_{s,m}$.

The model is trained with a mixed subject- and mesh-level objective:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L =
\lambda_{\mathrm{subj}}
\left(
\mathcal L^{\mathrm{subj}}_{\mathrm{stress}}
+
\lambda_r \mathcal L^{\mathrm{subj}}_{\mathrm{rank}}
\right)
+
\lambda_{\mathrm{mesh}}
\left(
\mathcal L^{\mathrm{mesh}}_{\mathrm{stress}}
+
\lambda_r \mathcal L^{\mathrm{mesh}}_{\mathrm{rank}}
\right)
+
\lambda_i \mathcal L_{\mathrm{id}} .
\end{equation}
Subject-level terms preserve the identity manifold defined by $D_{\mathrm{GT}}$,
while mesh-level terms expose the model to topology-induced variation. We use
$\lambda_{\mathrm{subj}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{mesh}}=1$, $\lambda_r=0.5$, and
$\lambda_i=0.25$.

\paragraph{Stress and ranking preservation.}
For embeddings $Z$ and valid pairs $\Omega$, latent and ground-truth distances
are matched with a scale-normalized Smooth L1 stress:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{stress}}
=
\frac{1}{|\Omega|}
\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}
\operatorname{SmoothL1}
\left(
\frac{\lVert z_i-z_j\rVert_2}{\mu_z+\epsilon},
\frac{D_{\mathrm{GT},ij}}{\mu_{\mathrm{GT}}+\epsilon}
\right),
\end{equation}
where $\mu_z$ and $\mu_{\mathrm{GT}}$ are mean distances over $\Omega$. We apply
this loss at both subject and mesh level. We additionally enforce the ordering
induced by $D_{\mathrm{GT}}$ using a margin-based ranking loss. For subject
triplets $(i,j,k)$ satisfying
$D_{\mathrm{GT}}(i,j)>D_{\mathrm{GT}}(i,k)+\tau$,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L^{\mathrm{subj}}_{\mathrm{rank}}
=
\mathbb E
\left[
\max
\left(
0,\;
\gamma -
\left(
\lVert \bar z_i-\bar z_j\rVert_2
-
\lVert \bar z_i-\bar z_k\rVert_2
\right)
\right)
\right].
\end{equation}
The mesh-level ranking term applies the same principle to pairs of mesh
distances, with ground-truth ordering inherited from subject identities. We use
$\gamma=0.05$ and ignore comparisons whose ground-truth distance difference is
below $\tau=0.02$.

\paragraph{Identity consistency.}
Finally, $\mathcal L_{\mathrm{id}}$ penalizes the variance of different
mesh-realization embeddings around their subject mean $\bar z_s$, encouraging
topology robustness while the stress and ranking terms preserve the global
identity-space geometry. The full mesh-level ranking and identity-consistency
definitions are given in Appendix~\ref{app:mesh_level_ranking_loss}.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experimental_setup}

\subsection{REMESH Cross-Topology Benchmark}
\label{sec:remesh_benchmark}
We build REMESH as a controlled metric benchmark from canonical 3D face shapes generated by the Basel Face Model (BFM)~\cite{bfm}, a 3D morphable model. These meshes are not outputs of image-based reconstruction methods. Instead, they provide reference identity geometry with known dense correspondence, allowing evaluation metrics and registration pipelines to be meta-evaluated under topology, support and sampling changes.

Following the metric meta-evaluation perspective of
Sariyanidi et al.~\cite{10448898}, synthetic data is used here as a controlled tool rather than as an end task. Because dense correspondence is known in the canonical representation, we can define reference identity distances and measure whether candidate metrics preserve them when the observed mesh representation is changed. The source meshes are first represented in a common canonical coordinate system. All meshes are centered and rescaled to an approximately $[-1,1]$ coordinate range. The GT-ready dataset contains 4,999 full-resolution meshes, each with approximately 53k vertices and 106k faces. We extract a canonical facial region using a fixed vertex-index mask, yielding meshes with 23,470 vertices and 46,440 faces.

From these canonical meshes, we construct REMESH, a controlled cross-topology benchmark with 500 identities and six mesh realizations per identity, for a total of 3,000 meshes. The six realizations are: \textbf{Original}, the canonical cropped mesh;
\textbf{Noisy}, the original topology with Gaussian vertex noise; 
\textbf{Remesh}, a smoothed and quadric-decimated mesh with approximately 16.5k vertices;
\textbf{Crop}, a conservative boundary crop with approximately 21k vertices;
\textbf{Down8k}, a strongly decimated mesh with approximately 8.1k vertices; and
\textbf{Up60k}, an upsampled mesh with approximately 60.4k vertices.
Each realization preserves the same underlying identity while changing topology, resolution, sampling density, support, or local geometry.

All meshes are additionally normalized per mesh before any distance is computed, by
centering on the vertex mean and dividing by the largest absolute coordinate. This step is
what makes meshes of different scales comparable, but it is not a neutral choice: the
divisor is a single extreme coordinate and is therefore itself topology-dependent. We
quantify its effect, and report all geometric baselines under a second, area-based
normalization, in Supplementary Sec.~1; the ranking conclusions of this paper hold under
both, and the normalization used here is the stronger of the two for the geometric
baselines in aggregate.

Because all identities of REMESH come from a single morphable model, ``cross-topology''
throughout this paper means \emph{across mesh realizations of one model family}. Transfer
to a second model family is a separate question, addressed in
Sect.~\ref{sec:ood_transfer}.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol and Implementation Details}
\label{sec:evaluation_protocol}
We use a fixed subject-disjoint split: 400 identities are used for training and 100 identities are held out for evaluation. No held-out identity is used during training. Model selection is performed on the fixed held-out REMESH benchmark, and all reported REMESH numbers use this same subject-disjoint benchmark. We therefore refer to these results as held-out benchmark performance rather than as an independent test-set estimate.

For each held-out identity, all six topology variants are evaluated, yielding 600 evaluation meshes. We consider cross-subject comparisons only. For topology-pair analysis, each ordered pair $(\tau_a,\tau_b)$ is evaluated separately and same-topology pairs are excluded. This gives $6\times5=30$ ordered topology-pair settings. Each setting contains $\binom{100}{2}=4{,}950$ subject pairs, for a total of $148{,}500$ cross-topology mesh-pair observations.

Candidate distances are compared against the correspondence-based $D_{\mathrm{GT}}$ using Spearman correlation as the primary metric and Pearson correlation as a secondary metric, as defined in Sect.~\ref{sec:problem_formulation}. We evaluate clean meshes and four perturbation regimes: jitter, translation, rotation, and mixed. Unless otherwise specified, the REMESH perturbation scale is $\sigma=0.02$.

The reported model uses the \texttt{xyz\_dn} DiffusionNet encoder with latent dimension 256, width 128, four residual blocks, dropout 0.1, and mean--max pooling. Training uses mini-batches of 5 subjects with up to 6 realizations per subject. We train for 120 epochs with learning rate $10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-6}$, and gradient clipping at 1.0. Online perturbations are applied during training with probability 0.6, using translation, rotation, and jitter sampled with relative weights $4{:}2{:}1$.

\paragraph{Hardware and software environment.}
The main training and evaluation runs were performed on a workstation with an AMD Ryzen 7 9800X3D CPU
(8 cores, 16 threads), 123 GiB of RAM, and one NVIDIA GeForce RTX 5090 GPU
with 32 GiB of memory, using NVIDIA driver 570.211.01. The software environment
used Python 3.12.3, PyTorch 2.11.0, and CUDA 12.9. On this setup, one 120-epoch
training run takes approximately 24 hours, corresponding to about 12 minutes per
epoch. Bootstrap, correlation, and table-generation analyses operate on
precomputed pair-level distances and run on CPU. Exact reproduction commands are
included in the released artifact bundle.

\subsection{Why Alignment Hurts: Distance Compression}
\label{sec:distance_compression}
The degradation above is not a failure of ICP as a pairwise fitting algorithm,
but a mismatch between pairwise registration and identity-aware evaluation. For a
mesh pair $(M_i,M_j)$, registration estimates
\begin{equation}
T_{ij}^{*}=\arg\min_T d_{\mathrm{geom}}(T(M_i),M_j),
\end{equation}
so each pair is evaluated after a different transformation chosen to minimize
local discrepancy. For 3D faces, this can absorb identity-related morphology
such as facial depth, jaw profile, forehead shape, and local protrusions, making
different identities appear artificially more similar
(Fig.~\ref{fig:icp_identity_suppression}).

\subsection{Out-of-Distribution Transfer}
\label{sec:ood_transfer}
We finally evaluate transfer to FaceScape~\cite{facescape}, a separate mesh family unseen during REMESH training. The goal is not to claim universal zero-shot generalization, but to assess how much of the learned identity-ranking structure transfers beyond the source benchmark.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:faceverse_summary}, in the full-neutral FaceScape setting, the REMESH-trained model shows nontrivial zero-shot transfer: on 110 neutral-pose subjects and 5,995 subject pairs, the latent distance reaches Spearman correlation $0.532$ in the clean setting. However, Chamfer remains stronger, reaching $0.605$. Under mixed perturbations, the gap increases, with latent Spearman $0.477$ compared with $0.638$ for Chamfer. Thus, the learned metric does not collapse out of domain, but it does not outperform Chamfer in this within-topology FaceScape setting.

The harder FaceScape cross-topology setting is where the first version of this work
reported a clear limitation: representing each subject by a downsampled FaceScape mesh and
an independently remeshed 10k-vertex variant, the latent metric reached only $0.115$
Spearman against $0.598$ for Chamfer, which we read as a substantial cross-model domain
shift. A controlled decomposition shows that this reading was wrong, and that the
experiment confounded two changes: the morphable model family, and the rule used to build
the second topology of each pair.

The 10k-vertex mate in that experiment is a Poisson surface reconstruction, which closes
the boundary loops of its source, adds $\approx21\%$ surface area, leaves the original
support, and departs from its own source surface by a normalized Hausdorff distance of
$0.38$ --- against $0.088$ for the \texttt{original}/\texttt{remesh} pair the metric was
trained on. Rebuilding the mate with the REMESH \texttt{remesh} rule, and changing nothing
else, raises FaceScape cross-topology transfer from $0.057$ to $\mathbf{0.404}$; our
synthetic FLAME half of the benchmark reaches $0.406$ under the same rule, i.e. a second
model family costs almost nothing. Conversely, applying the Poisson construction to FLAME
drops it from $0.406$ to $0.109$, reproducing the apparent cross-model failure
\emph{without changing the morphable model at all}. The weakness is therefore robustness to
surface re-reconstruction; it is present in every domain including the training one, and it
is a different axis from cross-model transfer. Both axes are reported in Supplementary
Sec.~4, together with the observation that matching the mesh \emph{support} to the training
domain lowers rather than raises the correlation, because $D_{\mathrm{GT}}$ integrates over
facial regions a crop discards.

Few-shot FaceScape adaptation substantially improves this behavior. With 75 FaceScape fine-tuning identities and evaluation on 35 held-out subjects, the adapted model reaches $0.537$ Spearman in the clean cross-topology setting, outperforming Chamfer at $0.403$. It also remains stronger under translation with $\sigma=0.10$ ($0.486$ vs. $0.417$). These results suggest that the FaceScape gap is primarily a domain-shift issue rather than a fundamental failure of the learned metric, although robustness under stronger jitter remains limited. Full FaceScape adaptation and forgetting results are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:faceverse_adaptation}.

\section{Conclusions and Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
We investigated a key limitation of standard error-computation pipelines for
3D face reconstruction. Our experiments show that pre-processing and alignment
steps commonly used in registration-based pipelines can distort the global
structure of shape similarity, making distances less consistent across mesh
pairs and degrading identity-ranking fidelity. This occurs because pair-specific
surface-fit objectives can suppress identity-related morphology and non-uniformly
compress inter-subject distances. Therefore, reconstruction evaluation should
distinguish global canonical normalization from pair-specific alignment, and
should not assume that lower post-registration surface error implies better
identity-aware comparability.

We proposed an encoder-only, mesh-agnostic learned metric trained to make
latent-space distances follow correspondence-based shape dissimilarities. The
metric is designed for ranking-preserving evaluation, not calibrated physical
measurement: latent distances should be interpreted as relative dissimilarities,
not as millimeter- or centimeter-level errors. As with all learned metrics,
performance depends on the training distribution --- but our controlled decomposition of
the FaceScape results (Sect.~\ref{sec:ood_transfer}) locates that dependence more
precisely than a generic domain-shift statement would. Under a matched pair construction
the metric transfers to a second model family, including real scans, at essentially the
same level as to a second synthetic one; what it is not robust to is a mate produced by
surface re-reconstruction, and that weakness is present in the training domain as well.
Multi-domain training and augmentation with re-reconstructed realizations therefore target
the measured failure directly, and are the natural next step, together with calibration
and interpretability of the facial regions driving latent distances.

Three further scoping limits should be stated plainly. The benchmark contains only
\emph{neutral} expressions, so nothing here establishes behaviour under expression
variation. The identities are sampled from a morphable model and therefore inherit the
demographic composition of the data that model was fitted to; a metric trained on them
inherits it in turn, and we make no claim of demographic uniformity. Finally, the
DiffusionNet backbone is discretization-agnostic but \emph{not} invariant to rigid motion;
robustness to pose in our results comes from the training perturbations, not from an
architectural guarantee.

Although the method is intended for evaluation rather than identification, it
operates on facial geometry and could indirectly support identity-related
applications if misused. We therefore frame the release as a benchmark and
metric-evaluation tool, report its domain limitations, and avoid redistributing
licensed mesh assets directly.

\subsection{DiffusionNet Operators}
\label{app:diffusionnet_operators}
For every mesh realization, we precompute the surface operators required by DiffusionNet, including the mass matrix, Laplace--Beltrami operator, spectral eigensystem, and tangent gradient operators. Vertex coordinates encode the extrinsic position of the surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$, whereas the Laplace--Beltrami operators encode intrinsic surface geometry.

Under a rigid motion $T(x)=Rx+t$, with $R\in SO(3)$ and $t\in\mathbb{R}^3$, intrinsic distances, angles, and area elements on the continuous surface are preserved. Consequently, the continuous Laplace--Beltrami operator and its spectrum are invariant to global rotations and translations:
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{T(\mathcal{M})}=\Delta_{\mathcal{M}},
\qquad
\Delta_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_\ell=\lambda_\ell\phi_\ell.
\end{equation}
This provides a useful geometric bias for robustness to global pose changes.

However, topology changes still affect the discrete operators. Remeshing,
decimation, subdivision, cropping, and noise modify the sampled mesh and
therefore change the discrete mass matrix, Laplacian, eigenbasis, and gradient
operators. Thus, intrinsic operators do not guarantee topology invariance by
themselves. Our model combines extrinsic XYZ coordinates with geometry-aware
diffusion operators and is explicitly trained across topology variants, so that
topology-dependent operator differences are treated as nuisance variation while
identity-related geometric differences are preserved.

\subsection{Training Details}
\label{app:training_details}

The reported model uses the \texttt{xyz\_dn} encoder with latent dimension 256,
hidden width 128, four DiffusionNet blocks, dropout 0.1, and mean--max pooling.
Training uses mini-batches of 5 subjects with up to 6 mesh realizations per
subject. The model is trained for 120 epochs with learning rate $10^{-4}$,
weight decay $10^{-6}$, and gradient clipping at 1.0.

The training objective is defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:training_objective}. In all
reported experiments we use $\lambda_{\mathrm{subj}}=1$,
$\lambda_{\mathrm{mesh}}=1$, $\lambda_r=0.5$, and $\lambda_i=0.25$.

Mesh-level supervision uses cross-topology pairs only. Ranking losses sample
1024 constraints per batch, with margin $\gamma=0.05$, tolerance $\tau=0.02$,
and 70\% hard constraints.

During training, online perturbations are applied with probability 0.6. The
perturbation modes are translation, rotation, and jitter, sampled with relative
weights $4{:}2{:}1$. Jitter magnitude is sampled log-uniformly between
$5\times10^{-4}$ and $2\times10^{-2}$. Rotations use angles between
$0.5^\circ$ and $12^\circ$, and translations use per-axis standard deviation
between $0.001$ and $0.03$.
\subsection{Mesh-Level Ranking Loss}
\label{app:mesh_level_ranking_loss}

At the mesh level, we compare two valid mesh pairs $a$ and $b$ and enforce the
ordering induced by their corresponding subject-level ground-truth distances:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L^{\mathrm{mesh}}_{\mathrm{rank}}
=
\mathbb E_{(a,b)}
\left[
\max
\left(
0,\;
\gamma -
\operatorname{sign}
\left(
D_{\mathrm{GT}}^a-D_{\mathrm{GT}}^b
\right)
\left(
d_z^a-d_z^b
\right)
\right)
\right],
\end{equation}
where $d_z^a$ and $d_z^b$ are the latent distances of the two mesh pairs. We
ignore comparisons whose ground-truth distance difference is below $\tau=0.02$.

The identity-consistency term used in the main objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{id}}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal S|}
\sum_{s\in\mathcal S}
\frac{1}{M_s}
\sum_{m=1}^{M_s}
\left\|
z_{s,m}-\bar z_s
\right\|_2^2 .
\end{equation}

\section{FaceScape Adaptation and Forgetting}
\label{app:faceverse_adaptation}

\subsection{FaceScape Target-Domain Construction}
\label{app:faceverse_construction}

FaceScape~\cite{facescape} is used as an external target domain to assess how the
REMESH-trained metric transfers to a mesh family unseen during source training.
No FaceScape identities are used for REMESH training, checkpoint selection, or
hyperparameter tuning.

We consider two FaceScape evaluation settings. The first is a
\emph{full-neutral} setting, where each subject is represented by one neutral-pose
mesh. We select files matching \texttt{*\_01.npz}, yielding 110 subjects and one
mesh per subject:
\begin{equation}
N_{\mathrm{subjects}} = 110,
\qquad
N_{\mathrm{pairs}} = \binom{110}{2} = 5995.
\label{eq:faceverse_subject_pairs}
\end{equation}
Each mesh has approximately 10k vertices and 20k faces, with DiffusionNet
operators precomputed. Pairwise latent distances are compared against a
FaceScape subject-level distance matrix computed from clean FaceScape detail
meshes using mean per-vertex Euclidean distance.

The second is a harder \emph{cross-topology} setting. Each FaceScape identity is
represented by two mesh realizations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Downsampled FaceScape}: the original FaceScape mesh reduced to
    approximately 10k vertices.
    \item \textbf{Remesh10k}: an independently remeshed version of the same
    subject, also with approximately 10k vertices.
\end{itemize}
Both variants include precomputed DiffusionNet operators. The downsampled
FaceScape meshes contain on average 10,152 vertices and 20,000 faces, while the
remeshed variants contain on average 10,113 vertices and 20,000 faces.

The remeshing pipeline samples points uniformly from the source surface,
estimates and orients normals, performs Poisson surface reconstruction, crops the
result using a scaled source bounding box, and finally applies quadric decimation
to obtain approximately 20,000 faces. This creates a two-realization
cross-topology FaceScape benchmark with 110 subjects, 220 mesh samples, 5,995
subject pairs, and 11,990 mesh pairs.

\subsection{FaceScape Perturbation and Chamfer Protocols}
\label{app:faceverse_perturbation_protocol}

We evaluate FaceScape robustness under five synthetic perturbation scenarios:
clean, jitter, translation, rotation, and mixed. For the fixed-noise experiment,
we use $\sigma=0.05$. Rotation uses a maximum angle of $1+20\sigma$ degrees,
giving $2^\circ$ at $\sigma=0.05$. Translation uses per-axis standard deviation
$0.002+0.05\sigma$, giving $0.0045$ at $\sigma=0.05$. The sigma sweep evaluates
\begin{equation}
\sigma \in \{0.00, 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.15, 0.20\}.
\label{eq:faceverse_sigma_sweep}
\end{equation}

The Chamfer baseline is evaluated both without ICP and, in a separate protocol,
with post-perturbation ICP. The no-ICP protocol uses raw Chamfer distances
computed directly between the evaluated meshes. The ICP variant aligns each
perturbed pair before Chamfer computation using 2048 ICP points, maximum
correspondence distance 0.05, and 20 ICP iterations.

held-out subjects and subject-pair mean aggregation.

model. These results motivate future adaptation strategies that explicitly
control forgetting, such as mixed REMESH--FaceScape rehearsal, distillation from
the REMESH checkpoint, partial freezing of the encoder, or multi-domain training.
